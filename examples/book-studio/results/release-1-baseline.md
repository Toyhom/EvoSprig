# Book studio — baseline

**Chapter 1: Know Your Numbers**

Start with a two-column list: fixed expenses (same every month) and variable expenses (they change). Your income is $2,400.

Fixed: rent $900, phone $45, transit pass $80. Total: $1,025.
Variable: groceries $310, eating out $140, utilities $95, fun $120. Total: $665.

Fixed plus variable equals $1,690, leaving $710 unaccounted for—likely small purchases you forgot. That gap is the point: you can't manage what you don't measure.

**Exercise:** Open your bank and card statements from last month. List every expense in the two columns and total each. Compare the grand total to $2,400. Where did the difference go?

**Chapter 2: Build Your First Budget**

Start with a simple split of your $2,400: 50% needs ($1,200), 30% wants ($720), 20% savings ($480). Needs cover rent, utilities, groceries, and transport. Wants are dining out, streaming, and hobbies. Savings is your buffer and future fund.

Now adjust to reality. If rent alone is $1,100, needs will exceed $1,200—trim wants or find a cheaper place. The percentages are a starting point, not a rule. A budget that fits your actual costs beats a perfect ratio you can't follow.

**Exercise:** Draft a monthly budget for $2,400 using the 50/30/20 split. Then list what you actually spent last month in each category. Compare the two and note one change you'll make.

**Chapter 3: Stay on Track**

A budget only works if you check it. Set a recurring 10-minute weekly check-in—same day, same time. Open your two-column list and compare this week's spending to your plan. Look for one thing: categories running hot. If dining out hit $90 by Wednesday against a $120 monthly plan, you've spotted it early enough to adjust—shift $40 from entertainment or cook at home for a week. No guilt, just data. Small corrections weekly prevent month-end surprises.

**Exercise:** Put a 10-minute check-in on your calendar for each week this month. At the first one, review your $2,400 budget and record one adjustment you'll make—for example, "Move $30 from wants to groceries."

# Editorial Handoff: Household Budgeting Onboarding Guide

## Introduction
This three-chapter guide introduces a recent graduate to household budgeting using a single fictional monthly income of $2,400. Each chapter is under 150 words and includes a practical exercise. The tone is direct and non-judgmental, and the guide deliberately avoids recommending financial products. The handoff below covers reading order, consistency corrections, and facts that need sourcing before publication.

## Reading Order
The chapters build sequentially and should run as written:
1. **Know Your Numbers** — establishes the two-column fixed/variable framework and the $2,400 baseline.
2. **Build Your First Budget** — introduces the 50/30/20 split and the reality-adjustment principle.
3. **Stay on Track** — adds the weekly check-in habit and course-correction.

Chapter 2 assumes the reader has completed Chapter 1's expense inventory, and Chapter 3's exercise references "your $2,400 budget" from Chapter 2. Keep this order.

## Consistency Corrections
- **Fixed-cost figures conflict across chapters.** Chapter 1 lists rent at $900; Chapter 2 uses $1,100 as a hypothetical ("If rent alone is $1,100"). This is defensible as an illustration, but consider labeling it explicitly as a different scenario to avoid confusing readers tracking one set of numbers.
- **Utilities placement.** Chapter 1 classifies utilities ($95) as *variable*; Chapter 2 lists utilities under *needs*. These aren't contradictory (needs vs. fixed are different axes), but a one-line note clarifying that "needs" spans both fixed and variable costs would prevent misreading.
- **Category overlap.** Chapter 1's "eating out" ($140) maps to Chapter 2's "wants" (dining out), and "fun" ($120) maps to wants/hobbies. Make the mapping explicit so the Chapter 2 exercise ("list what you actually spent in each category") is unambiguous.
- **Unaccounted gap.** Chapter 1's $710 gap is intentional and well-framed, but confirm the arithmetic: $1,025 + $665 = $1,690; $2,400 − $1,690 = $710. Correct as written.
- **50/30/20 arithmetic.** $1,200 / $720 / $480 sums to $2,400. Correct as written.
- **Chapter 3 example.** "$90 by Wednesday against a $120 monthly plan" and "shift $40 from entertainment" are internally consistent; no change needed.

## Facts Needing Sources
- **The 50/30/20 split** is presented as a standard starting framework. Attribute it (e.g., to its popularizer) or label it as a common rule of thumb rather than an established standard.
- **"You can't manage what you don't measure"** is a quoted management adage; either attribute it or set it as unquoted paraphrase.
- **Claim that weekly check-ins "prevent month-end surprises"** is a reasonable assertion but presented as fact; soften to "help prevent" or cite behavioral-budgeting evidence.
- **The 10-minute check-in cadence** is a recommendation without cited basis; frame as a suggested habit, not an evidence-based interval.

## Final Note
No financial products are recommended anywhere in the text, satisfying the brief. Verify all dollar figures against the $2,400 baseline after any edits, and keep each chapter under the 150-word limit during revision.
