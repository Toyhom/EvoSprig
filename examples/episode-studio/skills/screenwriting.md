# From a story to a shootable episode

Finish the character and location bible before writing shots. For each character, fix age range, silhouette, face, hair, clothing layers, two main colors, one distinguishing prop, temperament and speaking rhythm. For each location, fix geography, entrances, landmark objects, time, weather and key-light direction. Use these same details in every later description. Distinguish facts from the source from visual details you choose to fill gaps.

Work from desire → obstacle → choice → consequence. A scene changes something: a belief, a relationship, a physical situation. Put that change into an action the camera can see. Preserve the source's ending. An idea may need an invented ending; an adaptation does not.

Example idea: “A lighthouse keeper receives a letter from the sea.”

- Keeper: older woman, narrow silhouette, silver braid, ochre raincoat, dark boots, brass key on a red cord; clipped sentences that soften when she is alone.
- Lighthouse kitchen: circular stone room; west window at screen left; iron stove at right; chipped blue cup beside the door; dawn light enters from the left.
- Beat 1, 8 s: wide static view; a wet envelope slides under the door. The keeper notices it. Only surf and paper scraping.
- Beat 2, 10 s: medium side view; she takes off one glove and opens it. A drop falls onto the empty page. No speech.
- Beat 3, 10 s: close-up; writing appears where the drop lands. She whispers, “You remembered.” Hold her reaction.
- Beat 4, 8 s: wide exterior; she opens the lantern shutters toward the water. End on the answered signal.

Example adaptation: “The student wants to leave her village, but cannot tell her friend.” Keep the friend's identity and the source's resolution. Show her folded travel map under a schoolbook; let her stop mid-sentence when the friend asks about tomorrow. Do not turn unspoken thoughts into invented factual dialogue. Narration may carry source meaning that cannot be shown economically.

Before handoff: verify every speaker ID, location ID, outfit and prop; total the shot durations; read every line at a natural pace. Reserve roughly 3–4 Chinese characters per second for reflective narration and 4–5 for conversation. Split a long line at its natural clause boundary. Give a character's first spoken introduction a single-speaker shot where practical, so its approved audio can establish a reusable voice. Do not rearrange the story merely to obtain a voice sample.
